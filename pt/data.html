Why two numbers
Aircraft noise can be measured two legitimate ways:

1. Lden is the EU / Portugal regulatory standard (Environmental Noise Directive 2002/49/CE, RGR Art. 11) — it averages sound energy over day, evening and night periods, weighting evening and night events more heavily, and is compared against a legal limit of 65dB.

2. Peak + duration is what Lisbon Finals measures directly — how loud each individual pass gets, and how long it stays above 70dB, repeated roughly — times a day.

Neither is "more correct": Lden is the number regulators use to set limits; peak+duration is closer to what a person actually experiences during a single overflight.

LVA — Italian standard (OpenSky-verified)—
LVA (Livello di Valutazione Aeroportuale) is Italy's national event-based airport noise standard — instead of averaging all ambient sound, it counts and weights individual aircraft noise events. This event-based philosophy isn't unique to Italy: many countries use similar approaches (SEL — Sound Exposure Level, an international ISO standard used by the FAA, ICAO and UK airports; threshold-crossing event counts elsewhere). Italy has simply codified it into this specific national regulation.
All figures on this page are single-day calculations — not the multi-year representative averages official EU strategic noise maps are built from.

Reading the gap
A single loud pass barely moves a 24-hour Lden average — but it's very much part of the lived experience below the flight path. That's the point of showing both: Lden tells you what the regulator sees over a full day; peak + duration tells you what any single overflight actually sounded like.

How to read this
Needle
Circular-mean wind direction per 3h slot, from the raw degree reading (not the 16-point label, which hides in-between values).
Spread
Circular standard deviation: small means steady wind, large means it was actively turning within that slot.
Wind vs heading
Compares the wind against the real average aircraft heading tracked that slot, not a fixed runway bearing, so it stays correct whichever runway end was active that day. A "*" means no flight in that slot had heading data, so it fell back to runway 02/20's own bearing as an approximation.
Traffic
Arrivals/departures come from the same per-flight direction field used elsewhere on the site. Most days here are almost entirely arrivals, but active runway configuration (itself largely wind-driven) occasionally flips a day to mostly departures.
Weather data
Temperature/pressure read "—" before 11 Sep 2026 ~22h: the weather logger only started capturing them that evening, though wind speed/direction go back to 7 Sep.

Quick reference: decibels

The decibel (dB) scale measures sound intensity. It isn't linear — every +10dB roughly doubles how loud it feels, even though it means ten times more acoustic energy. Here's what different levels actually feel like:

Level Perception Everyday example
0 dBThreshold of hearingAnechoic chamber
10–20 dBExtremely quietRustling leaves, calm breathing
30–40 dBVery quietBedroom at night, library
50–60 dBQuiet / moderateNormal conversation, quiet office
70–80 dBLoud (tiring over time)Vacuum cleaner, heavy traffic
85 dBHearing-risk thresholdProlonged exposure starts to matter
90–100 dBVery loudLawnmower, car horn, headphones at max
105–110 dBImmediate riskRock concert, chainsaw
120 dBPain thresholdAircraft takeoff at 100m, F1 circuit
140 dBExtreme dangerGunshot, explosion
Did you know?
The decibel scale is logarithmic: a +10dB increase means 10× more acoustic energy, which the ear perceives as roughly twice as loud. So a normal conversation (60dB) actually carries about 1,000× more acoustic energy than a quiet bedroom (30dB) — which is why hearing fatigues so fast past 85dB.
Quick reference: vibration

The milli-g (mg) scale measures acceleration — how much the structure actually moves when an aircraft passes overhead. Unlike decibels, it's linear: twice the mg is twice the acceleration, felt directly through the floor rather than heard. Here's what different levels feel like on this terrace:

Level Perception Example
< 1.5 mgBelow the human perception thresholdNo one would notice
1.5–20 mgJust perceptible — softA distant or high pass
20–50 mgClearly feltA typical landing pass
50–80 mgNoticeable shakeA closer, heavier aircraft
≥ 80 mgStrong, unmistakableA loud widebody directly overhead
Did you know?
Unlike sound, which travels through the air, this is the building itself moving — sensed through the floor, not the ears. The ~1.5mg threshold (row one) comes from ISO 2631-1 / BS 6472-1, the standard for human perception of building vibration. Everything above that is this station's own classification, built from thousands of real measured passes.
